Cardiac timing from the heart sound itself

Formal thesis title: “Cross-Modal Reconstruction of Cardiac Signals: Decoding ECG-PCG Electromechanical Coupling Dynamics” (NUST, 2026).

Heart sounds record the mechanical consequences of each beat. A murmur’s grade, timing and shape are read against the cardiac cycle, but a stethoscope recording carries no ECG to mark it. This line of work asks whether the recording can supply that timing itself, and how far timing obtained that way can be trusted for unseen patients and new devices.

Question
Where should cardiac-phase boundaries come from when a stethoscope recording has no simultaneous ECG, and how much of the value of expert-reviewed segmentation can an automatic method recover for unseen patients?
Method
Thesis and the manuscript under revision at the IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics (JBHI): conditional reconstruction of an ECG-like signal from heart sounds. Manuscript under revision at the European Heart Journal – Digital Health (EHJ-DH), revised October 2026: a pre-registered, participant-grouped comparison of cardiac-phase boundary sources on the public CirCor DigiScope dataset, with one feature extractor, one classifier recipe and one fold split, so that only the boundaries differ.
My contribution
Developed reconstruction and phase-analysis workflows, designed experiments and patient-grouped evaluation, led the first-author manuscripts and a blind cross-device audit of the reconstruction.
  1. 2024 – Aug 2026

    The hypothesis

    A murmur recurs on every beat at the same cardiac phase; interference in the same frequency band does not. If an ECG-like reference could be reconstructed from the heart sound, its R-peaks would mark each cycle and phase-wise features would characterise the murmur. A conditional generative adversarial network (GAN) with multi-scale attention was built for that reconstruction (the JBHI manuscript), and the gating idea was presented at ESC Congress 2026.

  2. Oct 2026

    The patient-grouped result

    Re-running the murmur analysis with every patient’s recordings in one fold, and preprocessing fitted on training data only, showed that expert-reviewed phase boundaries carried information equal-time windows missed, while phase boundaries fixed at the reconstructed ECG’s R-peaks did not separate from equal-time windows. Separately, a blind test of the reconstruction on 338 record pairs from different hardware did not transfer. The earlier thesis and congress results are superseded.

  3. Oct 2026

    A pre-registered redesign

    For the journal revision, the question, the arms, the hypotheses and the margin were written down and frozen before any arm was scored; the plan is to be released with the paper. The comparison sets learnt phase boundaries, an acoustic segmenter and expert-reviewed segmentation against equal-time windows through one shared pipeline, so that only the source of the boundaries differs. Grouping is by participant, the dataset’s unit of identity: every recording from one participant stays in one fold.

  4. Revised manuscript (R1) · under review

    What the comparison found · to follow

    The revised manuscript (R1) has been submitted to the European Heart Journal – Digital Health and is under review. Its findings will be summarised here once the review concludes.

  5. Doctoral question

    What I ask next

    The route that worked on one dataset did not transfer to new hardware. Where does a timing model stop being reliable, how can it tell, and what must a signal pass before a downstream model may use it? My doctoral direction.

Finding to date · October 2026: In the October re-evaluation, with every patient’s recordings kept in one fold, expert-reviewed phase boundaries carried useful information for murmur characterisation, and the thesis’s synthetic-ECG route, with phases fixed at its reconstructed R-peaks, did not separate from equal-time windows. A pre-registered comparison of learnt, acoustic and expert-reviewed boundary sources (analysis plan frozen before any arm was scored; to be released with the paper) was submitted with the EHJ-DH revision (R1, under review) and supersedes that re-evaluation (not accepted or published); its findings will be summarised here once the review concludes.

What the revision tests: whether phase boundaries learnt from the heart sound alone recover the value of expert-reviewed segmentation, against equal-time windows and an acoustic segmenter, for unseen participants. How this leads to my doctoral question

Data roles, evaluation and limits

Paired ECG–PCG recordings support reconstruction development; separate-device paired recordings test reconstruction transfer. The public paediatric CirCor DigiScope heart-sound dataset is the classification cohort for murmur evaluation, with participant-grouped folds, macro-F1 for each task and paired participant-level bootstrap uncertainty. These are distinct data roles, rather than one shared clinical cohort.

The blind cross-device audit of the reconstruction covered 338 record pairs recorded with a different stethoscope at a different chest site; agreement did not transfer without retraining, and under matched hardware the reconstruction gives approximate cardiac-cycle timing, not a diagnostic ECG.

The pre-registered October 2026 comparison submitted with the EHJ-DH revision supersedes the thesis and congress analyses and the unregistered re-evaluation earlier in October 2026, whose qualitative conclusions are stated above; the thesis and congress results should not be cited as evidence for synthetic-ECG timing. Open: transfer to other devices and cohorts, and an external test of characterisation. The timing these methods supply is a phase reference for analysis, not a diagnostic ECG.

Probabilistic models of electromechanical coupling

I am developing copula-based coupled hidden Markov models (HMMs) to relate ECG events to heart-sound phases within each beat. Exploratory analysis tests how dependence changes with noise and whether event-level alignment captures relationships that pooled sample correlation misses.

Implemented so far
Exploratory analysis of synchronous ECG–PCG recordings using spectral, correlation and rank-based dependence measures; the coupled model remains in development.
My role
Develop the dependence analysis and coupled-model formulation, linking P-wave, QRS-complex and T-wave states with the first heart sound (S1), systole, the second heart sound (S2) and diastole.
Next test
Compare event-aligned dependence with pooled sample measures under matched noise and recording conditions.

Learning from heart sounds

My two first-author conference papers address distinct heart-sound classification tasks. They use different datasets, populations and evaluation protocols, so their results are not directly comparable.

ICAI 2023 · Paediatric congenital heart disease (CHD)
I trained a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN) on filtered four-second heart-sound segments, with pitch-shift augmentation, for binary normal/abnormal congenital-heart-disease detection from locally collected paediatric recordings and a small public set.
EUSIPCO 2024 · Five-class PCG
I developed band-selective pitch augmentation and a 1D CNN classification workflow using mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) for a separate valvular/normal heart-sound benchmark.
Clinical acquisition
I led the recording sessions with the paediatric cardiology team at two tertiary hospitals and six schools, wrote the collection procedures, consent documents and clinical questionnaires, and worked on echocardiography-confirmed annotation.

Hospital and school recording experience shaped my approach to acquisition quality and labels. Both are offline benchmarks, each on its own dataset.

Doctoral direction

Cardiac timing
under distribution shift.

I work on the measurement side of cardiac deep learning: the cardiac timing recordings can supply, and how far it can be trusted. My thesis asked whether a heart-sound recording can mark its own cardiac phases; a model that worked on one dataset did not transfer to new hardware, and that result set my direction. In a PhD I want to study cardiac-timing models under distribution shift: how a model can tell that a recording, device or population lies outside validated conditions; how much timing error each clinical reading tolerates; and how models stay calibrated as devices and cohorts change. I bring field acquisition, patient-grouped and pre-registered evaluation and cross-device testing, and want to learn continual learning and physiological modelling.

Research interests

Cardiac-phase timing from single-sensor recordings

Learnt segmentation of heart sounds, cross-modal timing from PCG to ECG, and how much boundary error each clinical reading tolerates.

Reliability under distribution shift

Patient-grouped and cross-device evaluation; detecting recordings that lie outside a model’s validated conditions; calibration across devices, sites and populations. Continual and domain-adaptive learning are the methods I want to learn.

Signal-quality gates for downstream models

Event-level acceptance checks (beat timing, interval error) that a reconstructed or denoised signal must pass before a classifier or a patient-specific physiological model uses it.

Wearable ECG–PPG timing

The ECG-to-pulse delay and its pre-ejection component, motion sensitivity, and calibration across users, on a research prototype; no blood-pressure claim.

Paediatric murmur characterisation

Grade, timing and shape from screening recordings, toward referral support where echocardiography is out of reach.

The thesis and its revision in 140 words

Heart sounds carry a murmur’s grade, timing and shape, but reading them needs the cardiac cycle, and a stethoscope recording has no ECG to mark it. My thesis asked whether the recording can supply that timing itself. A conditional GAN with multi-scale attention reconstructed an ECG-like reference from heart sounds on paired adult recordings; a blind test on different hardware did not transfer. Under patient-grouped evaluation, phase boundaries fixed from the reconstructed ECG did not separate from equal-time windows, while expert-reviewed boundaries carried useful information. The revised study, pre-registered before any arm was scored, compares learnt phase boundaries with equal-time windows, an acoustic segmenter and expert-reviewed segmentation on the public CirCor dataset. Earlier thesis and congress results are superseded. The open question is where such timing stops being reliable, and how a model can tell.
